How algorithms actually work
— and what PHX should build

A plain-language breakdown of what Spotify, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram and Netflix are really doing, which techniques exist, how much data each one needs before it works at all, and three concrete options for PHX. Written for a founder, detailed enough for a developer.

01What an "algorithm" actually is

An algorithm is not artificial intelligence. It's a scoring formula. For every possible thing you could show someone, you calculate a number, then you sort by that number. That's the whole idea. Everything else is detail about how the number gets calculated.

The thing people call "the TikTok algorithm" is literally an equation that was leaked to the New York Times in 2021, and it looks like this:

score = P(like) × V_like + P(comment) × V_comment + E(watch time) × V_watch + P(play) × V_play P = predicted chance you'll do that thing E = expected amount (seconds watched) V = how much the company decides that action is WORTH

Two halves. The P values are predictions — that's the machine-learning part, and it's a commodity. The V values are hand-typed by humans in a meeting. That second half is a values statement, not a technical decision. When Facebook weighted "reshares" 30× a like in 2018, that one number is what made the platform angrier. They quietly cut it to 1.5× in 2020.

So when you ask "should we add an algorithm," the real question is: what do we want to be worth the most? That's a question only you can answer, and I'll ask it properly at the end.

The universal three-stage shape

Every single platform below uses the same pipeline. YouTube named it in 2016 and everyone copied it:

1. CANDIDATE GENERATION millions of items → a few hundred fast, cheap, sloppy. "what's even eligible?" 2. RANKING score those few hundred carefully this is where the P × V formula lives 3. RE-RANKING / POLICY the rules you apply on top no two songs by the same artist in a row, force in something new, remove stuff they hid
Where PHX sits: with 6 tracks, stage 1 is SELECT * FROM tracks. Your entire game for the next year is stages 2 and 3 — and stage 3 (the rules layer) is where your brand actually lives.

02What the giants do — and the one thing to steal from each

SpotifyMusic

Not one algorithm — a portfolio. A "bandit" system called BaRT decides which shelf appears where on your homepage. Underneath, three separate engines feed it: what people play together, what people write in playlist titles and blogs, and what the audio physically sounds like. Discover Weekly is 30 tracks rebuilt every Monday; there's no way to pitch into it. A skip before 30 seconds is a strong negative signal; a skip near the end is roughly neutral.

Their cold-start answer for unknown artists is the important part: they trained a neural net to listen to the audio and predict where a song would sit in the recommendation space, so a track with zero plays is still recommendable on day one.

STEAL: audio-based cold start · editorial + algorithm hybrid ("algotorial")
TikTokShort video

Two bets nobody else made. First, it ignores who you follow — TikTok states outright that follower count is not a direct ranking factor. It's an interest graph, not a social graph. That's why an unknown creator can go viral, which is exactly the property PHX needs for unknown Phoenix artists. Second, they rebuilt the training system (published as Monolith) so the model updates from your behavior in minutes instead of days.

Their #1 signal is stated plainly: watching something all the way through outweighs everything else. New videos get shown to a small test audience; if completion and share rates clear a bar, it gets promoted to a bigger pool. Repeat. That's a bandit with successive elimination — and it's cheap to copy.

STEAL: completion rate as king · small-test-audience promotion · no two in a row from same creator
YouTubeVideo

The most-copied recommendation paper ever written (Covington et al., 2016). Two neural nets in sequence — one narrows millions to hundreds, one ranks those carefully. The famous lesson: they stopped optimizing for clicks because it produced clickbait, and switched to expected watch time. That single change was worth more than most model improvements.

Their most transferable trick is called "example age." Models trained on history are structurally biased against new content — they learn what was popular, which by definition excludes what just came out. The fix is one line: feed the age of each training example in as a feature, then set it to zero when you're actually recommending. The model then predicts "how hot would this be if it were brand new."

STEAL: freshness correction · the "shown to you and ignored" penalty (demotes what you keep scrolling past)
InstagramFeed & Reels

The clearest published version of the P × V "value model." A dozen small models each predict one thing — will you like it, comment, share, watch it through, hide it, report it — and those predictions get multiplied by business weights and summed. Mosseri stated the top three signals in 2025: watch time, then likes, then sends. Notably comments did not make the top three.

The single most useful recent finding for a small team: Meta discovered that engagement ≠ satisfaction, so they started asking people directly — a one-question survey on a small fraction of sessions ("does this match your interests?" 1–5). Training on that lifted model accuracy from 59% to 71% and engagement 5.2%.

STEAL: the 👍/👎 "was this a good rec?" survey — at your data volume, one explicit answer is worth ~100 implicit signals
NetflixVideo

Their insight: the whole page is the recommendation — not just the titles, but which rows appear, in what order, and even which thumbnail image you personally see. Every row on the homepage is a different algorithm (Trending Now, Because You Watched, Continue Watching, each its own model).

They're candid that the system struggles with brand-new members, which is why signup starts with a taste survey. And they publish a metric PHX should adopt outright: Effective Catalog Size — how much of the catalog actually gets watched. Personalization made members reach ~4× more of the library. For a platform whose mission is getting local artists heard, that's the number that proves you're working.

STEAL: shelves as the unit · signup taste picker · Effective Catalog Size as a north star

What all five have in common

  • Every one predicts several different actions and combines them with hand-set weights. Nobody optimizes a single number end to end.
  • Every one has an explicit freshness correction, because a model trained on the past is automatically biased against anything new.
  • Every one applies diversity rules as hard constraints after ranking — not as part of the math, as a rule bolted on top.
  • Every one's cold-start answer is ultimately content features + humans + forced exploration. There is no clever math that escapes this.

03The technique ladder — and how much data each needs

This is the part that matters most for you, because most of these cannot work yet. Recommendation techniques have hard data floors. Below the floor they don't degrade gracefully — they output noise that looks like a working feature.

The most important number in this whole document. A 2026 study tested LLM-based ranking against the dumbest possible baseline — just sorting by popularity. Popularity won by 33× (hit rate 0.268 vs 0.008), was 360× faster, and worst of all the LLM collapsed exposure: across 500 users it recommended only 3 unique songs as the top result. For a platform whose entire mission is spreading plays across local artists, that failure mode is fatal. Separately, an engineer benchmarked a real Postgres recommender on 800k interactions: content embeddings lost to popularity, and a plain GROUP BY co-occurrence query beat the embeddings by 3.4×. Simple wins. Ship SQL first.
Where LLMs genuinely do help: not in the live ranking loop, but offline — auto-tagging your catalog (genre, mood, "sounds like" — about a tenth of a cent per track), generating weekly playlist ideas for you to approve, writing the "why you're seeing this" copy, and turning a typed vibe ("dusty desert night drive") into a search. The LLM does language; the database does ranking.

04The honest truth about PHX right now

You have 6 tracks, 2 artists, and 14 streams.

There is no algorithm on earth that produces a meaningful recommendation from that. With 6 tracks you can put all 6 on one screen. Collaborative filtering needs overlapping behavior between many users, and you have essentially none. The LightFM script sitting in /ml cannot work — archive it.

So the right move isn't "add an algorithm." It's instrument now, curate now, and climb the ladder as the numbers grow. Every company above will tell you the same thing: the model is a commodity, the behavioral log is the moat.

The single most urgent gap: PHX logs plays but not impressions — what was shown and not played. Without a denominator you cannot compute a single rate: not completion rate, not click-through, nothing. YouTube's most predictive feature was literally "how many times has this been shown to this user and ignored." This is ~2 days of work and everything else depends on it. If you do one thing from this document, do this.

Your actual competitive advantage is not algorithmic

It's the Phoenix scene graph — which artists play the same venues, share bills, run in the same collectives, who's playing Friday at Valley Bar, which neighborhood a listener lives in. Spotify cannot build that for Phoenix and never will, because there's no money in it for them. Encoded as ranking features, that data will outperform any model you could train, and it's hand-buildable at 2 artists and still works at 200.

05Three options for how PHX's algorithm should work

★ Recommended

Option A — "The Curated City"

Hand-built shelves that a light algorithm personalizes. Netflix's structure at 1/1000th the complexity. You define 5–8 named rows — New This Week in Phoenix · Playing Live This Weekend · Because You Follow [Artist] · Deep Cuts (under 50 plays) · Sounds Like [track] · Staff Picks — and each one is a SQL query. The "algorithm" decides which rows you see first and what order items sit in inside them.

Every feed reserves slots: roughly 14 proven · 4 exploration · 2 editorial out of 20. Every new track gets a guaranteed floor of impressions before the system is allowed to judge it — TikTok's test-audience mechanic, which doubles as a marketable promise: "every track gets its first 200 listeners."

Effort~7 days
Data needednone
Runs inSupabase SQL
Explainablecompletely
Add at ~100 users / 100+ tracks

Option B — "Sounds Like Phoenix"

Option A plus audio fingerprinting. Every track gets run through a music-embedding model (CLAP or MuQ-MuLan) once on upload, producing 512 numbers describing how it sounds. Stored in Postgres via pgvector. This solves cold start completely — a track uploaded five minutes ago with zero plays is instantly recommendable next to things that sound like it.

It also unlocks two things for free: semantic search ("downtempo desert night drive" typed as text, matched against audio), and onboarding taste picking — pick 3 artists at signup and you get real recommendations in your first session. Cost is roughly zero; it's a one-time batch job that runs on a laptop.

Effort+10 days
Data neededa catalog, not users
Runs inpgvector + 1 script
Cost~$0
Add at ~1,000 users

Option C — "The Interest Graph"

Behavioral signal finally comes online. Upgrade the existing co-occurrence recommender with proper normalization (so it stops just returning the most popular track every time), run the same query over follows — which accumulate faster than plays — and hand-build the artist knowledge graph: same venue, same bill, same collective. Then fit small prediction models for each objective and let them replace the hand-typed guesses in the scoring formula.

This is where you'd also add an evaluation harness, because past this point you cannot tell whether a change helped without measuring it. Rule from the research: if your fancy model doesn't beat plain popularity on a holdout test, ship the popularity baseline.

Effort+15–22 days
Data needed~10k interactions
Runs inSQL + nightly worker
Prereqimpression logs

07The part nobody warns you about

Engagement-optimizing design is being actively litigated right now. The federal social-media addiction case (MDL 3047) has ~2,900 pending cases; Snap and TikTok settled bellwethers in January 2026, and in March a jury hit Meta and Google for $6M including punitive damages. Section 230 protects you as a publisher of other people's content — it does not protect your own product design. Arizona is a bellwether district.

The specific features being called defective read like a list of what not to build: infinite scroll, variable-reward notifications, autoplay, streaks, public follower counts, engagement-optimized feeds, and weak age gating.

The good news: every defensive choice here also makes PHX better and costs nothing today. Finite named sets that actually end ("This Week's 25," "Tonight in Phoenix") are easier to rank well with little data. Notifications on real events (an artist you follow dropped something) instead of "we noticed you haven't opened the app." Diversity floors that stop one artist eating the feed — which is your mission anyway. A chronological toggle. And an honest "why am I seeing this?" that shows the actual arithmetic — which you can only do because you chose simple algorithms. Nobody at Spotify's scale can be that honest. It's the same "with receipts" brand promise, applied to discovery.

08What I need you to decide

These are the calls that are yours, not mine. They set the V values in the formula — the values statement.

1. What should the feed optimize for?

Options: time spent (what everyone else does, and what's being litigated) · artists discovered per person · follows and saves · money reaching artists · tickets and merch clicked. My recommendation is a blend led by discovery breadth, because it's the only one that matches "we get local artists heard" and it's defensible in a courtroom.

2. How aggressively do we force exposure for new/small artists?

A guaranteed impression floor is a fairness promise you can market, but it costs feed quality. Options: every track gets its first 100 / 200 / 500 listeners guaranteed — or no floor, purely merit-based.

3. Does an artist's payout ever affect their ranking?

Spotify sells this (Discovery Mode: lower royalty rate in exchange for algorithmic promotion). It's real revenue and it's also exactly the kind of thing PHX's brand is positioned against. My strong recommendation is never — and to say so publicly, loudly.

4. Do we ship "why am I seeing this?"

It's a genuine differentiator you can only offer while the algorithm is simple, and it extends the receipt brand into discovery. Costs about a day and a half at Stage 1.
